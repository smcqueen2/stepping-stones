# Ogmore, its crossing and its landscape: historical research

Research date: 24 October 2026. Prepared for a single-page, click-through historical timeline. On 6 November the owner requested publishing this work; the researched content and paintings are now integrated into the app's History view. The original design alternatives remain archived alongside this brief.

## Approach

The research prioritises Cadw's visitor and designation records, the Royal Commission's Coflein records, Heneb's historic environment/landscape records, an official historic guide and a Welsh Government guardianship record. The surrounding-area narrative includes Ewenni Priory, Merthyr Mawr and Candleston, rather than treating the castle as an isolated building. Modern conservation sources are dated to the events they report.

The full proposed short copy, longer context, date qualifications and source mapping are in [timeline-content.json](timeline-content.json). It supplies both design options, keeping the history identical when comparing layouts.

## Findings that affect the design and wording

### The rivers must be named correctly

The stones cross the **Ewenny**, close to its confluence with the Ogmore. The castle is on the southern/southeastern bank. Cadw's site and crossing descriptions establish this. Keep the wider Ogmore-valley identity while avoiding labels that put the stones over the wrong river.

### Foundations are a period, not a securely dated building day

Current Cadw interpretation uses the early 12th century. Documentary references anchor William de Londres at the site in 1116; archaeological landscape descriptions sometimes give approximately 1100. These are compatible levels of precision. The preview says “Early 1100s” and explains the 1116 anchor. It does not turn a first written reference into proof that the building was founded that year.

### Building phases need room to develop

The keep, cellar, curtain wall and later heightening were not all built at once. The surviving early keep began as a two-storey structure and was later raised; a cellar belongs to the later 12th century, with the major enclosure/gateway phase in the early 13th. The drawings are therefore labelled interpretive. Exact missing roofs, fenestration and domestic ranges require archaeological/architectural checking before treating any image as an accurate reconstruction.

### The Lancaster marriage has a source discrepancy

Radford's official guide, in the 1974 reprint consulted, gives 1297; Cadw's listed-building description and Coflein give 1298. Use the late 1290s/late 1200s in display copy and retain the discrepancy in the source notes. Henry of Lancaster should not be confused with his son Henry of Grosmont. The castle's Crown connection in 1399 is part of this longer ownership story; it is not the modern guardianship agreement.

### Local work matters alongside nobles and walls

The Coflein fulling-mill record cites fourteenth-century manorial accounts for a mill beside the castle, but says its exact location is unknown. Heneb records changes in the settlement around the rebellion. These sources support a working-valley narrative. They do not support painting a watermill at a precise invented spot, or staging a detailed battle at the castle.

### The stones cannot be pinned to a romantic founding date

Cadw GM184 calls them probably medieval, with later revisions. Its description records 41 stones, whereas other modern publications give different totals. Avoid an unqualified live stone count. A story about a de Londres daughter or a suitor is folklore, not proof of who placed the stones or when. The current surviving alignment also should not be inserted into every early reconstruction.

### Candleston's fate needs qualification

The medieval settlement's loss under sand and the fortified residence's longer occupation should not be merged into a single disaster. The scheduled structure is a fourteenth-century tower house/fortified manor. It is not evidence for a twelfth-century residence of the same form. The wider dunes have a long, changing archaeological and ecological history.

### 1803 is a record of continued use

The courthouse outlasts the main castle. The proposed wording uses the recorded survival of court activity, without claiming a documented last hearing or closure ceremony on a particular date.

### Guardianship is not ownership or a lease

The 2023 Welsh Government response confirms the 1928 date and explains the care/public-access arrangement. The historical milestone says guardianship begins, with modern responsibilities described separately. It does not place Cadw or Welsh Ministers in existence in 1928 or claim a purchase then.

### Modern management accounts also age

The NRW 2020 announcement records a lease ending; it should not be recycled as proof that NRW continues to manage the reserve alone. The 2023 report is by the Estate's land agent, a party involved in the arrangement. The timeline presents both as dated events, avoiding an unverified statement about every operational detail in 2026.

## Folklore as a companion, with its own label

The preview includes **Hawise and the common**, explicitly labelled local folklore and left outside the dated sequence. A Welsh Government/Visit Wales 2016 workshop pack retells the daughter, prisoner and common-land story. It also demonstrates why variants cannot be treated as a family genealogy: names, relationships and the Candleston setting do not align neatly with the archaeological chronology. The preview summarises the tradition without presenting it as the legal origin of the common.

The White Lady tradition is an additional candidate for later editorial work. Marie Trevelyan's *Folk-lore and Folk-stories of Wales* (1909) is a useful original collection to check. A scan was located, but the downloaded copy has no usable text layer for the targeted extraction, so it was not used as the authority for an extra legend in these designs.

## Sources and research files

- Cadw visitor pages and statutory records are linked above and individually in the content data.
- Coflein NPRNs 93019 (castle) and 414237 (fulling mill) provide archaeological/historical record descriptions and bibliography.
- C. A. Ralegh Radford, *Ogmore Castle*, official guide, reprinted 1974, prepared by the Department of the Environment for the Welsh Office. The linked public scan and its text layer were read during research. Downloaded scan/extracted text remain local and are not redistributed in this archive. The historical guide is evidence with a date, not the final word on every later interpretation.
- [Britain from Above, WAW033689, 1950](https://britainfromabove.org.uk/en/image/WAW033689) is a useful archival view of castle and stones. It is linked for future visual research; the archive photograph has not been reproduced or treated as freely licensed.
- The narrative deliberately omits an unproven Roman fort at Ogmore, the “Twelve Knights” tradition as a literal founding document, exact medieval stone-count claims and unverified scenes of a specific siege.

The new paintings are illustrations, not archival photographs, maps or certified archaeological reconstructions. Each chapter carries the appropriate image label.

The two proposed layouts are **Living landscape** (closest to the existing immersive scene) and **Painted chapters** (a more editorial page-turning composition). Both keep the history on one page and move horizontally on explicit milestone/previous/next selection. No auto-play, no vertical scroll-driven camera and no change to the app's existing live scene.

## Preview checks

The final self-contained preview was checked in the in-app browser at desktop width and at 360/320px. Date selection, Previous/Next, the native mobile milestone picker, chapter/source expansion, separately labelled folklore and design switching work. The last milestone disables Next; inactive chapters are inert and hidden from assistive technology. Slide transitions honour reduced motion. Caption layout grows with copy and the design text-size/width controls, rather than clipping longer text into a fixed-height box. Browser logs contained no warnings or errors.

The archived preview embeds five compressed WebP paintings and the full sourced content, totalling about 703 KB. There is no external library or data request. `build-preview.mjs` rebuilds that review from `timeline-template.html`, `timeline-content.json` and the saved assets. The application now imports the same canonical content and paintings in a separate lazy History module; it uses Living landscape with desktop date buttons, a mobile milestone picker, source notes and the folklore companion. The archived previews preserve the earlier two-layout comparison.
